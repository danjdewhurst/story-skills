## Chapter Text

At eleven minutes to midnight, with the last Edinburgh train gone and the cleaners already working their machines across the concourse towards her, a woman in a green coat put a blue suitcase on Ines Achebe's counter.

"One week," the woman said. She paid in coins, counting them out as if each one cost her something. "And don't open it. Whatever it says."

Ines wrote the ticket. 4417. She tore it along the perforation, gave the woman the top half, and slid the counterfoil under the elastic band on the handle. Then, because it was what she did on slow nights, she made a small pencil tick on the back of the woman's half, in the corner, where nobody would ever look.

The woman did not say goodbye. She was gone through the arches before Ines had lifted the case onto shelf nine.

It was light for its size. Something inside shifted when she set it down, and settled.

*Whatever it says.*

Ines worked nights at the Harrowgate Street left-luggage office because nothing happened there. People left bags. People came back for bags. In between she read, and drank tea from the flask her mother still filled for her, and listened to the station go quiet around her like a house.

At twenty past one, the bell on the counter rang.

The man was wet from the shoulders up, though it had not been raining when the woman came in. He laid a ticket on the counter and turned it round so she could read it.

4417.

"My wife's," he said. "She left something for me. The name's Sallis." He smiled the way people smile at cash machines.

Ines picked up the ticket. She turned it over, the way she always did, and tilted it towards the lamp.

No pencil tick.

"I'm sorry," she said. "This isn't the ticket I issued."

"It's the right number."

"It is the right number." She put it down. "It isn't the right ticket."

For a moment Mr Sallis did nothing at all. Then he took the ticket back, folded it once, and put it in his breast pocket.

"You'll be here tomorrow night," he said. It was not a question.

When he had gone, Ines sat for a long time with her hands flat on the counter. Then she went to shelf nine, slid the counterfoil out from under the elastic band, and turned it over.

On the back, in a hand she had not seen since she was eleven years old, someone had written her name.
