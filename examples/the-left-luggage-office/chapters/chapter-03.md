## Chapter Text

Ines caught Pat Dunmore at the top of the arches, under the clock, with his car keys already out.

"Who signed for 4417?"

He looked at the keys, then at her. He had worked at Harrowgate Street for thirty years and he had never been a good liar; it was one of the reasons she liked him.

"She had the ticket," he said. "The proper half. Your little tick was on the back. I know you do that, I'm not daft."

"She signed my name."

"She asked me to book it out under you." He rubbed his face. "She said, 'Then she'll know it was me, and nobody else will.' And she said a man might come asking, and if he did, I wasn't to know anything." He reached into his coat. "She left you this."

The envelope was sealed. Inside was a key, old and heavy, with a brown luggage label tied to it. On the label, in slanting blue capitals: PARCELS. SHELF 9. HE WILL COME BACK.

"The old parcels office?" said Dunmore. "That's been shut since your dad retired."

"I know," said Ines. "That's why."

* * *

At three in the morning, with the station dark and the last cleaner gone, she let herself through the door at the end of platform two.

The old parcels office smelled of dust and engine oil. Her torch found the racks, the brass numbers, the pigeonholes where her father had once sorted other people's post. Shelf nine was empty except for one thing.

A ledger. Green cloth, the corners worn white. PARCELS RECEIVED 2013.

She opened it on the counter. Most of the pages were in her father's handwriting, careful and dull: dates, weights, initials. In April the pages stopped being dull. The same consignee every week, four weeks running, and every parcel signed out by the same initials.

R.S.

Then a page was missing. Not torn: cut, close to the spine, with something very sharp.

On the page after it, the last entry her father ever made in this book was a single line.

*F. knows. Don't let him have it.*
