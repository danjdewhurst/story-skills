The light was going when I knelt at Ana's sea-chest. The lamp could wait ten minutes. It had waited on worse.

I moved the spare mantles off the lid and put the brass key in the lock. It went in to the bow. It would not turn. I eased it, then leaned on it, then leaned harder, and the grey went out of the window while I worked it. Somewhere below, the rock went dark.

It snapped. A small sound, like a twig. I had the bow in my fingers and the bit was in the lock, and the chest was as shut as it had been for four winters.

I sat back on my heels. My hands were shaking, so I put them on my knees.

Then I lit the lamp, because that is the job, and I trimmed it, and I sat with the chest.

Two ways, and neither of them clean. Force it, and break what Ana kept shut. Or leave it, and never know why somebody climbed my stairs to hang a key where I would find it.

I would not break it.

Petra's boat was due Thursday. The smith in Skerry could draw the bit and open it with care. Petra would see the chest go down the steps. She would ask. I would take it ashore on Thursday all the same.
